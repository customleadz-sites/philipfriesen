What this means

The math that matters: one job pays for everything. A click costs about $9.65, a call about $80, a won job about $268 — against jobs worth $2,000–$10,000. Even the minimum budget only needs one job a month to come out well ahead.
That ≈$80-per-call figure isn't a guess. It's what an actual dirt-moving client of ours pays per call today, and this region's own bid data lands in the same place. The estimate and the real-world result agree.
Competition out here is on your side. The biggest phrases are rated Low-competition — bid data even thins out because so few companies advertise. The exception is the Dallas side of the radius, where clicks get expensive; we'd start targeting the rural counties and only expand toward the metro if results support it.
The buckets above become the campaign structure. Each service — general excavation, dirt work, grading and pads, trenching, ponds — gets its own ad group and its own landing page (landing pages are included with management). Specific ads beat generic ones on both price and results.
Expect the ramp, not instant results. Google's algorithm needs conversion data to find the right people. Month one may run near $100–125 per call; by month three it should be at or under the $80 mark. The budget you choose sets how fast it learns.

How we corrected the numbers. The service area (30 counties within two hours of Paris) exceeds what Google reports at once, so demand was pulled in three ten-county batches and combined — no county counted twice. Then four honesty passes: (1) we removed 155 phrases that don't apply — competitor company names, equipment and machinery searches (excavators, skid steers), engineering jargon, and price-shopping queries; (2) phrases Google reports at "10 searches/month" are rounded up — we count each as 3; (3) Google counts the same search under many similar phrases, so duplicate variants were merged (e.g. "excavation contractors near me" was being counted five ways); (4) a further 30% discount on the total, because Google's own documentation says volumes overlap and independent studies find Keyword Planner overestimates. No number here double-counts, and nothing is inflated to make ads look better than they are.
